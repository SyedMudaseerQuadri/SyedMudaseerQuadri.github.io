Turning a page you read
into something you keep.

Daily Recall scans a page from a book and turns it into a summary, a word game and spaced flashcards — closing the gap between highlighting something and actually remembering it.

壱The signal

A personal annoyance that turned out to be everyone's

It started the way a lot of usable products do — with a problem I was living. My reading habit was leaking; I'd finish a book and keep almost none of it. Before designing anything, I checked whether the pain was mine alone.

Those view counts weren't opinions — they were a crowd quietly raising their hands. The same question, asked by millions, across unrelated creators. My private frustration was a widespread, under-served need. That was the green light.

弐The incumbent

The existing tool didn't earn the effort it asked for

Someone had tried to solve this — I found Readwise and genuinely wanted it to be the answer. The value was there in theory, but the experience cost more than it returned. Rather than trust my own reaction, I interviewed nine people from the target audience.

The complaints clustered: a flow that felt heavier than the payoff justified. I stopped guessing and documented every friction point users hit as they moved through it. That list became my brief.

参The advantage

I didn't let the design die as a clickable prototype

Instead of handing over a static mockup and waiting, I designed the app and then rebuilt it as a functioning one — not a mockup pretending to work, but something I could put in a hand and watch get used.

That changed my feedback loop entirely. People stopped imagining how a flow would feel and started tapping through it — failing and succeeding at the exact friction points I'd catalogued.

四The iteration

Two full cycles — the surviving version, not a lucky guess

I re-interviewed, redesigned, re-interviewed and redesigned again — two complete cycles, each grounded in watching real behaviour rather than my own taste. The capture flow got simpler; the recall surface got tuned until reviewing felt lighter than the reading it came from.

The final design wasn't my first idea. It was what was left after the weak ones got tested out.

五The takeaway

The pixels were never the job

The design file stayed the source of truth and I stayed the design authority — AI was just a faster brush. The real job was noticing a genuine pain, proving the demand behind it, refusing a tool that didn't deserve its users, and iterating against real reactions until the product fit. That's the same loop I run on client work — and the one I'll bring to your project.

A colour change that spreads
like a symbiote.

T Vastra makes embroidered, oversized tees and sells them through Instagram. They wanted a black, premium online store with good type and micro-animations on the product cards. I directed and designed it, then built it as a working store with AI as the brush: Claude Code for the build, OpenAI Codex as an independent reviewer. The venom idea, the critique and every keep-or-cut call were mine.

Request a demo walkthrough Let’s connect → The working store is private to the client. Ask and I'll walk you through it live, venom effect included.
壱The brief

Black and premium, without looking like a template

The ask was a black, premium store with good fonts and micro-animations on the shopping cards, built on placeholder data so the real catalogue could drop in later.

One constraint shaped everything: the client didn't like their own logo's colours, so the palette couldn't come from it. I built it from the product instead: near-black, a warm bone white, and a single red taken from the thread already in their spider designs.

弐The signature

Picking a colour shouldn't be a swap. It should spread.

Most stores just replace the photo when you pick a colour. I wanted the change itself to feel like the brand, so I designed what I called the venom effect: a black, tendril-edged layer bleeds out across the shirt, the new colour chases it, and the embroidery re-threads itself halfway through.

That decided how it was built. Photos can't do this, so every tee is drawn as live SVG: the colour is its own layer and the tendrils come from a noise-distorted mask. Twelve designs, sixteen colourways, one renderer. Anyone who has reduced motion turned on gets an instant swap instead.

参The card

Every tap answers back

The product card is where people decide, so every action has its own feedback. The colour name slides to the new one, Front / Back flips the shirt in 3D, and Add to bag runs a spinner into a hand-drawn check while a dot in the shirt's colour flies into the bag and the bag icon bounces. The heart bursts when you save a tee.

The error state is designed too. Tap Add without a size and the sizes shake while the button itself turns red and says "Pick a size first". That version exists because the first one, a floating label, overlapped the colour swatches. I caught it while taking these screenshots and fixed it before publishing.

四The iteration

The direction I tried, and walked back

After the first build I judged parts of it generic, with too many familiar moves. So I explored a quieter, logo-led direction: a Roman serif matched to the wordmark, square corners, no intro, a centred hero.

Side by side, the bold original sold the product better; the quiet version made the tees feel smaller. I rolled it back but kept what the second pass got right: the client's logo, cut cleanly out of the flat JPG they sent, now in the nav, the intro and the footer and stitched onto a tee in thread colour, plus the emblem as a faint backdrop behind the hero shirt.

五The handover

A store, not a mockup

It works end to end: the bag survives a page reload, quantities update with a running subtotal, and a bar fills toward free shipping. Before handing it over I put the code through an independent review by a second AI system (OpenAI Codex) and scripted real-browser tests. The first review found four issues and the second found four more. All eight are fixed and the final review came back clean.

Honesty was part of QA too. The placeholder reviews are labelled as samples, and product specs I couldn't verify are flagged for the client to confirm before launch. A demo that pretends to be real is a liability, not a deliverable.

Review — eight bugs caught before the client saw them

Found by Codex across two review rounds, fixed, then re-checked in a scripted Chrome session.

  1. Front/Back flip: a second tap mid-animation was ignored. It now retargets.
  2. Flip retarget: the shirt snapped flat before turning. It now turns from its current angle.
  3. Bag drawer: keyboard focus could leave it. It is now a modal dialog and the page behind is inert.
  4. Toast button: "View bag" stayed reachable behind the open drawer. It is removed while the bag is open.
  5. Closing the bag: focus went back to a button that had disappeared. It now returns to the bag icon.
  6. Fast open/close: a timer could focus the hidden Close button. The timer is cleared on close.
  7. Saved data: a corrupted bag in browser storage could crash the store on load. Every item is now validated.
  8. Duplicate items: two identical saved items became two linked rows. They now merge into one line.

Client project · gym management app Member + owner apps · one Flutter codebase

One QR at the door.
The whole gym on one phone.

Tone Zone Fitness, a gym, needed two things from one app: members who can get in and pay without queueing at the desk, and an owner who can see who's inside, who's paid and who's waiting for approval without a notebook. I designed the dark Tone Zone design system and every screen on both sides, then built it in Flutter with AI as the brush.

Let’s connect → Screens are from the working Flutter build, running on demo data.
壱The brief

A gym app that costs nothing to run

The gym's flowchart split into two people: a member who needs a pass and a way to pay, and an owner who needs to check people in, approve new members and chase unpaid fees.

I made ₹0 running fees a design rule, not an afterthought. Login is email and Google, so there are no SMS costs or DLT registration. Payment is direct UPI to the gym's own QR, so there are no gateway fees. Cash payers aren't forgotten either: the owner can mark them paid in one tap.

弐The pass

The door check takes one glance

Each member gets a signed QR pass that rotates every 30 days. When the owner scans it, a card slides up with the member's name, ID, payment status, validity and last visit, topped by one big answer: Allow entry or not.

A pass can be screenshotted, so the card is built for the owner to check the person against the name rather than pretending the QR alone is proof, with a slot for a member photo. The QR carries no personal data, just a reference the server signs.

参The owner

A hidden back office in the same app

There is no "admin" button for members to find. The owner's tools only appear when the owner logs in: approve or reject sign-ups, freeze a membership, record a cash payment, or let someone in on a trial with an override.

The dashboard answers the questions an owner actually asks at the end of the month: how much came in, how many have paid, and exactly who hasn't.
